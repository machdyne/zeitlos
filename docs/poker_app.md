# poker: the table

The part of `sw/apps/poker` that draws. `docs/poker_engine.md` covers
the rules, the opponents and the tests behind them.

**Status: complete.** Engine, table, card art, keyboard, mouse, game
mode and the difficulty ladder all work, and the host test suite is
green: 588 checks across five binaries. Four variants, three betting
structures, eight difficulty levels.

## One renderer, two rectangles

The app has to work inside a window and full screen in game mode
(`docs/game_mode.md`), and those look nothing alike from the inside. A
window sits somewhere on a 640x480 desktop, is inset by the titlebar,
may be partly covered and may be resized at any moment. A game-mode
page is a fixed 320x240 at a known offset with nothing in front of it.

Everything in `table_ui.c` is therefore written against an **origin and
a clip rectangle in absolute screen coordinates**. Windowed passes the
window's content rectangle; game mode passes the back page. That is the
entire difference between the two modes.

This is `sw/apps/chess/board_ui.h`'s arrangement, deliberately rather
than coincidentally. The alternative — window-relative helpers for one
mode and raw framebuffer writes for the other — is two renderers that
drift, and the drift stays invisible until somebody switches modes
mid-hand and finds the table laid out differently on the two sides.

## Seats in rows, not around an oval

An oval is what a poker table looks like and it was tried first. At
320x240 it does not survive contact with seven-card stud: a seat needs
room for a name, a stack, a bet and up to seven cards, and eight of
those arranged on an ellipse overlap each other well before they
overlap the board in the middle.

Rows also keep the layout a pure function of the rectangle, which an
ellipse fitted to a resizable window is not.

The status line is anchored to the top; the command line, message line
and buttons to the bottom, because those are the parts being typed into
and clicked and they must not move when the window is resized. The pot
and board are **centred in what is left** — see "What the render
caught" below for why that is worth stating.

## The card art

The encoding, the tiles and the shoe moved to `sw/common/games` when
`sw/apps/blackjack` became their second caller — see
`docs/casino_bank.md`. What follows describes them where they now
live.

Generated by `sw/common/games/gen_cards.py` into a committed
`zcardart.c`, the same
arrangement `sw/apps/chess/gen_pieces.py` and `sw/data/icons` use: the
build needs no Python, and where the art came from is still obvious.

### Whole tiles, not sprites with a mask

A card is an **opaque white rectangle** and has to replace what is
under it. A masked sprite needs raster ops, which `zgfx.h` says to
probe for because an older bitstream silently treats every rop as
`COPY`, and it tears where there is no cookie-cut mode. One opaque
`z_fb_hw_blit_mem()` per card needs none of that.

### Two sizes, and why the small one is shaped as it is

**Full, 20x28**, for the hero's own cards and the community board:
rank, a small index pip, a large central pip.

**Mini, 12x17**, for the other players: rank over suit letter, both
inside columns 2 to 6. That placement is the whole design. Seven-card
stud gives a seat up to seven cards, and a six-handed table leaves
about 100 pixels of width per seat, so the cards are drawn
**overlapped at an 8-pixel stride** — exactly the way a hand of cards
is fanned. With the rank and suit both in the left five columns, every
card in the fan stays readable, and a full seven-card hand occupies 60
pixels.

### Suits on a display with no colour

On a full card, spades and clubs are **solid** pips and hearts and
diamonds are **hollow** ones. That works because the shapes differ as
well: a hollow diamond cannot be mistaken for a solid spade. The hollow
versions are computed from the solid ones by keeping only the pixels
that touch a hole, rather than drawn separately, so the outline is
always exactly the shape it pairs with.

On a mini card the pip would be four pixels across, where solid and
hollow are the same picture. So minis use the suit **letter** instead.

### The bit order is not the font's

`z_fb_hw_blit_mem()` reads its source with the **least** significant
bit leftmost (`zgfx.h`, `zbm.h`), the opposite of the MSB-first
convention `z_font_t` glyphs and `zicon.h` icons use. Get it backwards
and every card is mirrored — exactly the right size, exactly the right
place, passing every geometry assertion. `tests/test_layout.c` compares
a drawn card against its source tile bit for bit, which is what catches
it.

### Cost

9,540 bytes of read-only data for all 104 tiles and two backs. Against
`sw/apps/chess` spending 24KB on a transposition table out of the same
process allocation, that is affordable. Nothing is allocated at
runtime.

## Looking at it

    cd sw/apps/poker
    make render
    make render WHAT=showdown|stud|draw|allin|game|narrow

Writes `/tmp/poker.pbm`. Seven states, because six of them are a
nuisance to reach on hardware and one — a partially occluded window —
cannot be reached from inside the app at all.

`sw/common/tests/zrender.h`'s header explains why this exists: a
geometry assertion can only check a relationship somebody thought to
write down. For a card table most of what matters is in the other
category. Whether a rank is legible at twenty pixels. Whether an
overlapped fan of seven stud cards reads as seven cards or as a smear.
Whether a face-down card is instantly distinguishable from a face-up
one. Whether the hollow pips read as red or just as damage.

**Look at the output before changing the art or the layout.**

## What the render caught

Three defects that every assertion in the suite passed.

**Every card was a photographic negative.** The first version of
`gen_cards.py` drew the card body as 0 and the ink as 1. On this
display 1 is lit, so a card face is white and its ink is black — the
polarity `sw/apps/chess` already uses for its light squares. Obvious in
one look at a single card; invisible to anything that only checks where
the cards are.

**A band of dead space through the middle of the table.** With the top
anchored and the bottom anchored, all the slack pooled in one place —
about a third of the window in five-card draw. Every band was inside
the window, in the right order, not overlapping anything. It was just
ugly. Fixed by centring the pot and board in the space between the
opponents and the hero.

**The disabled buttons lost their labels** -- twice, and the second
time it shipped. They are shaded rather than hidden, because a control
that vanishes when it is unavailable is a control whose neighbours move
under the pointer. But the label must not be drawn *on* the shading.

**There is no grey on this display.** A dither is black pixels and
white pixels; nothing else. A white glyph over a light stipple has
almost no contrast, and a black glyph over a heavy one is black pixels
among black pixels -- the shape is simply gone. `zgfx.h` makes it worse
than a contrast problem: on a bitstream with no dither support,
`z_fb_hw_fill_shade()` falls back to a plain black or white fill, so
black-on-shade is sometimes black on solid black.

Both attempts were looked at in a 2x render before shipping. The second
looked *faint* rather than absent, and the response was to tune the
dither level rather than to stop drawing text on texture. **"Faint but
present" in a render is "unreadable" on the hardware**, and no amount
of staring at a PNG settles it.

The fix is not a level. A solid hole is cut back out for the label and
it is drawn white, exactly as an enabled one is; the shading surrounds
it rather than sitting under it. `tests/test_layout.c` asserts that a
disabled label is drawn with *the same pixels* as an enabled one, and
separately that the button as a whole did change -- so a renderer that
simply ignored the disabled state cannot pass either.

That episode also exposed a flaw in the harness rather than the app.
`zrender.h`'s shade is `((x + y) & 3) < level`, which saturates at
level 4 -- so every level `zgfx.h` defines from 4 to 16 came out solid.
`tests/poker_shim.h` now supplies a real 4x4 ordered dither over
`Z_SHADE_MAX`, so what the render shows is what the hardware will do.

And `z_fb_hw_fill_shade()` was being called with unclamped coordinates.
It clamps to the screen and knows nothing about the content rectangle,
exactly like `z_fb_hw_fill_rect()`; it was only safe because the
buttons sit well inside the window. There is a clipped `shade()` helper
now, and a test that draws into a deliberately narrowed clip -- what an
occluded window actually gets -- and checks nothing lands outside it.

## What the layout tests prove

    cd sw/apps/poker && make test        # includes test_layout

88 checks. The geometry: the bands stack in reading order without
overlapping, the buttons do not collide, the cards fit across the
width, the opponent cells tile the row, a seven-card stud fan fits
inside a seat, and both a window's content rectangle and a game-mode
page produce a usable table. The layout is also checked for being a
pure function — the same rectangle twice gives the same answer, and
moving the origin moves everything.

Then what actually lands on the framebuffer:

- **Nothing is drawn outside the content rectangle.** This is the one
  that earns its keep. `sw/apps/logic` shipped its panel wrong three
  times and all three were this — window coordinates used where content
  coordinates were needed. Checking every individual rectangle cannot
  catch the general case; drawing the whole thing at a non-zero origin
  and looking for ink where there should be none can.
- **Every band and every opponent seat has ink in it.** A band left
  blank by an arithmetic slip is invisible to the overlap assertions,
  which only ever said the bands did not collide.
- **A drawn card matches its source tile bit for bit**, and the art is
  verified not to be its own mirror image, so that comparison cannot be
  vacuous.
- **A half-clipped card shows its right half, not its middle.** When
  another window covers part of this one, wm narrows the visible region
  and the blitter is told a smaller rectangle — the source origin has
  to move with it. Nothing else in the suite ever clips a card, so this
  narrows the clip by hand, which is what an occluded window does.
- **The gap between two cards hits neither.** A hit test built on the
  wrong stride still puts every point inside card *i* inside card *i*;
  the gap is the only place a wrong stride disagrees. In five-card draw
  disagreeing means discarding the card next to the one clicked.

### Mutation testing

Five mutations of the renderer, each expected to break one check:
dropping the content-rectangle clamp, clamping a clipped card's
destination but not its source, using an ORing blit, skipping a seat,
and using the wrong card stride in the hit test.

Two survived the first pass and both were real gaps in the tests, now
closed: nothing ever clipped a card, and the hit test only probed
points inside cards.

A third survived for an honest reason worth recording. `pt_draw_all()`
clears the content area before it draws anything, so on that path an
ORing blit and a copying one produce identical output. The copying shim
is kept because it is what the hardware does, and because the moment
anything draws a card over existing pixels the difference becomes the
whole ballgame — but it is not currently load-bearing, and the test
that claimed to prove opacity was proving nothing. It was replaced with
the bit-for-bit tile comparison, which is a stronger statement and
catches the mirroring hazard as well.

## Playing

Type a command and press Return, or click. Both routes end in the same
place -- an action `pk_legal()` has accepted handed to `pk_act()` -- so
neither is a special case of the other, and `tests/test_input.c` checks
that folding by click and folding by command leave the game identical.

| Command | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| `f`, `fold` | |
| `c`, `call`, `k`, `check` | whichever is legal |
| `r [n]`, `bet [n]` | raise to *n*, or to the amount in the box |
| `120` | a bare number is a raise to that amount |
| `a`, `allin` | |
| `d 1 3 5` | throw those cards away (five-card draw) |
| `p`, `pat` | keep all five |
| `new` | re-seat everybody with fresh stacks |
| `deal` | next hand |
| `level 1-8` | difficulty |
| `variant holdem\|draw5\|stud5\|stud7` | |
| `seats 2-8` | |
| `limit nl\|fl\|pl` | no limit, fixed limit, pot limit |
| `odds` | your equity from here, as a percentage |
| `game` | full screen; Escape comes back |
| `help`, `quit` | |

`F1` steps through the help on the message line. `F2` toggles full
screen. `Escape` clears the command line, and stops an opponent
thinking. `-` and `+` step the bet amount by half the pot, the same as
the two small buttons.

### Why there is a command line rather than single-key actions

Single keys are tempting for a game with five things you can do, and
they collide immediately: `c` has to mean "call" and also has to be the
first letter of any command starting with c. Resolving that by "act on
the key unless the line is non-empty" makes typing `call` fold you on
the `c`.

So everything goes through the line, exactly as chess does. Single
letters *are* commands, so it is two keystrokes per action.

**One exception, and it is the one that is safe.** Return or Space on
an empty line **checks**, when checking is legal. Checking costs
nothing and cannot be regretted, so it gets the one-key path. Calling a
bet by accident is precisely the mistake worth designing out, so
nothing that costs chips has one. Between hands the same key deals
again.

### Amounts are "raise to", never "raise by"

The one genuine ambiguity in a poker interface. "Raise 100" means
different things at different tables; "raise to 100" never does. The
command, the amount box and `pk_act()` all use the same convention.

A raise larger than your stack is **rounded down to all in** rather
than refused -- somebody typing a number bigger than their chips means
all of them. A raise smaller than the minimum **is** refused, with the
minimum named, because rounding that one up would put in more chips
than were asked for.

## Difficulty

Eight levels, described in `docs/poker_engine.md`. They are bounded by
wall clock first and sample count second, so a faster board gets a
better estimate in the same two seconds rather than playing at a
different strength.

## Not freezing the desktop

**This was shipped wrong once, and it is worth reading before touching
the rollout counts.**

An opponent can think for a second or two, and this is a cooperatively
drawn desktop: an app that stops servicing its message queue stalls the
**window manager**, not just itself. So `pk_ai_decide()` gets a poll
callback that runs the message pump every few hundred rollouts and can
abort.

Two consequences, and they are the same two chess has. While an
opponent is thinking, input is restricted to window events and Escape,
because a command would change the game the estimate is being computed
from. And an unanswered `Z_WM_REDRAW` is what stalls the desktop, so it
is answered even in game mode and even mid-decision.

### How often, and the bug that came from getting it wrong

The first version polled inside two nested guards, `(r & 63) == 0`
around `(r & 255) == 0`. At the default level's 450 rollouts that fired
**once per decision**, and at levels 1 to 3 it never fired at all:

| Level | Rollouts | Polls per decision (before) | (after) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 60 | 0 | 4 |
| 2 | 120 | 0 | 8 |
| 3 | 250 | 0 | 14 |
| 4 | 450 | 0.9 | 28 |
| 8 | 3000 | 11 | 189 |

Six-handed, five opponents act before the person does, so the app
serviced about five messages in the several seconds after startup.

The symptoms were **a permanently blank window** and **wm timing out
waiting for the redraw acknowledgement whenever the window was moved**.
Both are the same cause, and the blankness is the part worth
understanding: `repair_region()` *clears* a window's content before
drawing chrome into it, then sends `Z_WM_REDRAW` and blocks in
`wait_for_redraw_done()`. So the app's own startup paint was wiped by
wm's chrome pass, and the redraw that would have restored it sat
unanswered.

Now the loop polls every 16 rollouts, `r == 0` included so that a
decision with fewer rollouts than the interval still pumps, and
`pk_ai_decide()` polls once up front so the weak levels' random path --
which answers without sampling at all -- services messages too.

The first poll can only pump; it cannot abort. An estimate built from
zero samples is not an estimate, and the caller would get the neutral
500 that means "no idea" rather than the rough answer a handful of
samples already gives.

`tests/ai_test.c` asserts a **rate** now, at every level. Nothing else
in the suite could have caught this: every other test either installs
no poll callback or installs one and asks only whether it was called,
never how often.

One difference from chess worth stating, because chess's equivalent
comment says the opposite: **repainting from inside the poll callback
is safe here.** A poker opponent does not mutate the game while it
thinks -- it deals private copies of the deck and never touches
`pk_game_t` -- so the table on screen is always the real one. Chess's
search makes and unmakes moves on the position it was given, which is
why it has to search a copy.

## Memory and size

Built for rv32i with section GC:

| | text | data | bss | total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| poker | 66,336 | 88 | 2,372 | 68,796 |
| chess | 58,004 | 116 | 42,824 | 100,944 |

Smaller overall than chess, which already ships. The text is about 8KB
larger, almost exactly the card art; the `.bss` is tiny because there
is no transposition table -- the rollouts are stack-only.

Nothing is allocated. An app's `malloc()` grows into its 16KB stack
allowance (`docs/app_runtime.md`), so anything of size has to be
static.

`make nmcheck` confirms at the object level what `make nofloat`
confirms lexically: not one soft-float helper is referenced by this
app on an rv32i build. `__divsi3` and friends **are** referenced and
are expected, since rv32i has no divide either.

That check is scoped to this app's own objects, and deliberately.
`sw/common/zobj.c` has a `float` member in `z_obj_t` and compares it
with `fabsf()`, so `zobj.o` pulls `__subsf3` into **every** app in this
tree on an rv32i build. That is a property of the shared object system
and a separate question from whether this app kept its own arithmetic
integer; failing here on somebody else's float would make the check
something people turn off. Worth knowing about regardless.

## A bug in `sw/common/zrng.c` that this app tripped over

`z_rng_below(n)` **never returns when `n` is a power of two.** It
computes its accept bound as

    limit = 2^32 - (2^32 % n)

which for such an `n` is exactly 2^32, and that truncates to **0** in
the `uint32_t` it is stored in. The loop is `do { v = z_rng_u32(); }
while (v >= limit)`, and on an unsigned `v >= 0` is always true.

A 52-card Fisher-Yates asks for bounds of 52, 51, ... 2, so it hits 32,
16, 8, 4 and 2 on **every** shuffle. Poker hung on its first deal,
inside `new_hand()`, before it had drawn anything. What that looked
like from outside was a permanently blank window, a window manager
timing out waiting for a redraw acknowledgement, and no response to any
key -- because the app never reached its message loop at all.

`sw/apps/repl/zapi.c`'s `(random n)` has the same exposure: `(random
16)` will hang the REPL.

Poker no longer calls it -- `poker.c` supplies raw words from
`z_rng_u32()` and `pk_rng_below()` does the bounding -- so nothing here
depends on the fix. The fix itself is small:

```c
uint32_t reject = (uint32_t)(0x100000000ull % (uint64_t)n);
if (reject == 0) return z_rng_u32() % n;   /* n divides 2^32 */
do { v = z_rng_u32(); } while (v >= (uint32_t)(0u - reject));
return v % n;
```

The case that has to be handled separately is the one the original
arithmetic cannot express: when `n` divides 2^32 there is nothing to
reject, and the accept bound would be 2^32, which does not fit.

## Installing

Four edits outside `sw/apps/poker`, left for you to make rather than
shipped as modified files, so nothing you have changed gets
overwritten.

**1. The app list.** Add `poker` to `APPS` in `sw/apps/Makefile`.

**2. The dock.** `sw/data/icons/icon-poker.png` is included. Then:

    cd sw/data/icons && python3 gen_dock_icon_data.py

which regenerates `sw/apps/wm/dock_icons.c` and `.h` from every PNG in
that directory. Add one line to `dock_candidates[]` in
`sw/apps/wm/wm.c`:

    { "poker",      z_icon_poker_data },

**3. The card image.** Add to the table in `release/lib/mkfatimg.py`:

    ("apps/poker", "sw/apps/poker/poker.bin"),

**4. Rebuild** `wm` and `poker`.

## Building and testing

    cd sw/apps/poker
    make              # the target binary
    make test         # five host test binaries, native cc
    make render       # draws the table and writes /tmp/poker.pbm
    make cards        # re-runs sw/common/games/gen_cards.py
    make nmcheck      # needs the RISC-V toolchain and a completed make
    make stress       # every layer at once under ASAN and UBSAN

`make test` builds the **shipped** sources with the host compiler.
There is no second copy of the evaluator, the betting engine, the
layout or the command parser anywhere in this tree.

`make stress` runs the layers together the way `poker.c` does -- deal,
opponents act, redraw after every step, hero acts through the command
line, settle, repeat -- across every variant, seat count and betting
structure, under `-fsanitize=address,undefined`. 1,097 hands and about
15,700 steps, clean. It is separate from `make test` because the
sanitizers make it slow, and it is the right thing to run after
changing anything that writes into a buffer.

## The bankroll

Poker buys in from `/user/casino.dat` and cashes out when the table
ends, sharing one bankroll with the rest of the casino
(`docs/casino.md`). **The boundary is the table, not the hand** -- a
stack rises and falls across many hands, so there is no round to settle
at the way there is in blackjack or slots.

**Nothing leaves the bank when you sit down.** The buy-in is recorded
and the chips stay in the file until the table is over, so a window
closed mid-hand costs nothing. What you are worth is the bank plus
whatever is in front of you; the status line shows both.

Busting settles immediately rather than waiting for `new`, because
otherwise closing the window would be a way to walk away from a loss.

**The opponents are not drawing on it.** `pk_game_init()` seats everyone
with a full stack, which is right for the other seats; the hero buys in
for what the bank can cover. A short buy-in against full stacks is a
real disadvantage -- which is what being short of money in a card room
is, so it is left as it falls.

## What is not here

- **No saved games.** A hand in progress is not resumable; only the
  bankroll survives a restart, and it does so at table boundaries (see
  below).
- **No Omaha.** `pk_eval_constrained()` exists and is tested -- the
  evaluator half is done -- but no variant uses it. It also wants
  pot-limit betting as the default, which the engine already supports.
- **The opponents draw plainly.** They bet well and, in five-card
  draw, discard everything below a pair rather than computing it.
  Choosing a discard properly needs the equity of every subset of the
  hand, which is 31 estimates where a betting decision needs one, in
  the one variant that would use it.
- **No hand reading.** Levels 7 and 8 track a fold frequency, not a
  range.
- **No memory of folded up-cards in stud**, which a strong human player
  would certainly use. The most worthwhile of these to close.
